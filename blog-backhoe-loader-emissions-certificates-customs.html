Emissions, Certificates and Customs: What Your Country Will Actually Ask For

Almost every second enquiry I receive contains the same three letters: do you have CE? And almost every time, the buyer asking it needs something other than what they named — because engine emissions, machine safety conformity and import paperwork are three separate files, issued by three separate bodies, requested at three separate moments. Which one your country wants decides the engine, the price and the lead time. In this article I separate the three, say plainly what our machines are built to and where they ship, show you how to tell a real certificate from a decorative one, and flag the paperwork that has to start while your machine is still on the production line — because the documents that get applied for after the ship sails are the ones that cost you demurrage.

October 29, 2026 · 19 min read

One Question, Three Different Files

Start here, because it is where most of the confusion is created. When a buyer, a tender document or a customs broker says "certification", they may mean any of these:

The same three letters can mean an engine certificate, a machine declaration, or a shipment document. They are not interchangeable.

Why it matters in practice: file 1 is decided by the engine manufacturer, before the machine exists. File 2 is decided by the factory's conformity work on that model. File 3 is decided by your country, per shipment, and expires with it. If you ask for "CE" and the supplier answers "yes", you have learned nothing unless you know which file they said yes to.

Emissions: What the Engine Is Built To, and What Your Country Enforces

Non-road diesel engines are regulated in stages, and the stages move at different speeds in different parts of the world — you will see Stage IIIA, IV and V in Europe, Tier 4 Final in the United States, India's own CEV stages, and China's own non-road stages. The same machine can be legal in one port and not registrable in another, with no change to the machine at all.

Two separate facts are easy to merge by accident:

  • What the engine was built and certified to. That is a property of the engine, documented by its manufacturer.
  • What your country enforces at import or registration. That is a property of your market.

The gap between those two is where import problems are born. A buyer in a market with no engine emission enforcement at the border will never be asked for an emission certificate. A buyer importing into a jurisdiction that requires an EPA or EU engine certificate will be asked for a document that no amount of negotiation can produce after the engine has been built — because it has to be issued against a certified engine family.

The single most useful sentence you can put in your first message to any supplier: "My country enforces ______ at import — is your engine certified to that, and can you show me the certificate number?" If the answer is vague, you have found the problem in week one instead of week nine.

A Higher Stage Is Not Automatically Better for You

This is the part suppliers rarely say and buyers rarely hear. It is tempting to read the emission stages as a ladder and to want the top rung. But a higher stage is not a free upgrade — it is a different machine to own.

Higher stages get there with after-treatment: diesel particulate filters, selective catalytic reduction, exhaust gas recirculation, and the sensors and control software that go with them. All of that works beautifully under the conditions it was designed for, and those conditions include:

  • Fuel quality. After-treatment systems are sensitive to sulphur and to contamination. Fuel that a simpler engine tolerates for years can damage a filter in a season.
  • Maintenance capability. Regeneration cycles, DEF/AdBlue supply, sensor faults and warning lights need a technician who has seen them before. In a market where the nearest qualified workshop is two countries away, a warning light that derates the machine is downtime, not a warning.
  • Duty cycle. Machines that idle a lot or work at low load behave differently with after-treatment than machines worked hard all day.

So the honest framing is not "standard versus premium". It is matching: the emission stage follows the regulation of the country where the machine will be registered, and beyond that, the simpler engine is often the better machine for the ground, the fuel and the workshop you actually have. There is one exception and it is not a preference — if your country enforces a stage at import or registration, that stage is a fact of entry, not an option.

I wrote about the same principle for configuration choices generally: there is no best configuration, only the configuration that matches the work and the country.

What Our Machines Are Built To, and Where They Ship

Our backhoe loaders leave Qingzhou with Chinese engines — Weichai, Yuchai and Xinchai depending on the model — built to China's non-road emission stage for new engines. That is the stage our domestic market requires, and it is the stage most of our export destinations accept: across Africa, the Middle East, South America, Southeast Asia and Central Asia, these machines import and register without an engine emission certificate being demanded at the border.

I want to be equally plain about the other case. If your country enforces EU Stage V or US EPA certification for non-road engines at import or registration, then the machine has to be built around an engine that carries that certification, and that is a different engine, a different price and a different lead time. It is also not something that can be solved at the end — a certificate cannot be retro-fitted to an engine that was never certified, and no sticker changes what came out of the factory.

Tell me the destination port in your first message. If the answer is "we can't do that one", you will hear it on day one, not after the deposit.

Machine Safety Conformity: a Different File Entirely

Set emissions aside for a moment. The second file is about the machine as a product: guarding of moving parts, stability, the cab structure, noise at the operator's ear, the manual, the warning labels, and the technical file behind all of it. In the European Union this is what the CE marking actually covers for machinery, and the rulebook has been moving from the Machinery Directive to the newer Machinery Regulation — which text applies to your import depends on the date and on your importer's own obligation, so confirm it with them rather than with me.

Here is the practical problem with "CE" as a question. A marking on a machine is a declaration by the manufacturer, backed by a technical file. It is not, on its own, a quality award. So when a supplier answers "yes, we have CE", the useful follow-ups are:

  • Who issued it, and in what role? A conformity assessment involving a notified body is a different thing from a certificate bought from a consultancy. Ask for the body's name and its identification number.
  • Does the declaration name this model? A certificate for one model does not cover a different one, and a certificate for a component does not cover the machine.
  • Can it be verified? Real certificates carry registration numbers that can be checked. Our own industry writes about this: buyers cross-check numbers against the issuing body's database and look for the supplier's company seal, and an unverifiable certificate is treated as a warning sign rather than a document. That habit is correct.

I will put our own position plainly. We supply machines to markets that do not require EU conformity assessment, and I do not describe what we can supply in language designed to sound like it. If your market needs a conformity assessment we have not done, I would rather say so and lose the order than send you a document that fails the first check your broker runs.

ROPS and FOPS: a Test Report, Not a Piece of Paper

There is one more safety item that comes up constantly in tenders and almost always gets filed under "certificates": the protective structure of the cab. Roll-over protective structures and falling-object protective structures are covered by ISO standards, and what demonstrates compliance is a physical test on that structure — a load applied to a cab, and a report recording what happened.

That distinction changes what you should ask for. Not "do you have ROPS" but:

  • Which standard is the cab claimed to meet?
  • Is there a test report, and is it for this cab design?
  • Does the cab carry a plate or marking identifying it, so the machine in your yard can be matched to the report?

This matters most where it is enforced — mining sites, large contractors, and government tenders in several Gulf and African markets, where a machine without the structure and its documentation is simply not allowed on site. It also matters to you regardless of enforcement, because that structure is what stands between an operator and the machine.

Pre-Shipment Conformity: the Programmes That Must Start Early

The third file is the one that actually stops containers. A number of countries run a pre-shipment conformity programme: before the goods sail, an appointed agency checks them and issues a certificate that your customs will ask for on arrival. The names differ by country — you will hear PVoC in Kenya, SONCAP in Nigeria, the SABER platform under SASO standards in Saudi Arabia, GOEIC registration for Egypt, and others — and the specific programme, the fees and the product scope all change over time.

I am deliberately not going to print a table of requirements here, because a table goes out of date and an out-of-date table is worse than none. What does not go out of date is the shape of the process:

  1. It is applied for before shipment, not after. The inspection happens on the goods, usually in the country of export.
  2. It is tied to that shipment. The certificate matches a specific consignment, so it has to be arranged per order.
  3. It takes calendar time. Application, document review, physical inspection, and certificate issuance each take days, and they run alongside production — not after it.
  4. Someone has to be the applicant of record. Usually the importer. That means your side's details have to be correct early.

If you are importing into the Gulf, I have written about the certificate-driven nature of that market in more detail in the Middle East buyer's guide. For everywhere else: name the destination port to your broker in week one and ask which programme applies, then pass that answer to your supplier before production starts.

Eurasia: EAC and the Cold-Start Question

Russia, Belarus, Kazakhstan, Armenia and Kyrgyzstan run the Eurasian Conformity system, and machinery entering that market is marked EAC. As with the European case, this splits into the machinery safety side and the engine emission side, and the emission stage recognised there is its own. If you are importing into that region, the two things to establish early are which conformity scheme your product falls under, and who is going to be the local entity of record — because schemes of that kind generally need a party inside the customs union to hold the certificate.

There is a second, non-paperwork point about that region that is worth more than most certificates: cold. A machine that starts at −25 °C is a machine with the right battery capacity, the right oil grade, glow plugs that work, and a fuel system that does not gel. I have written a separate piece on winter operation, and it is worth reading before you order rather than after the first frost.

HS Codes and the Declaration: Six Global Digits, Then Yours

Every import is declared under a tariff code. Construction machinery of this kind sits in heading 8429, and backhoe loaders are commonly declared under subheading 8429.52 — the same code referenced in our step-by-step import guide. The first six digits are harmonised internationally; everything after that is national, and it is the national line that sets your duty rate.

Three practical points:

  • Confirm the national line with your own broker before the bill of lading is issued. Correcting a declaration after arrival is far more painful than checking it first.
  • The declared value is your number, not mine. It is based on the commercial invoice, and it is your side's legal declaration. I will issue an accurate invoice; I will not issue one that says something else.
  • Duty and tax are set by your country. They vary widely by destination and by how the machine is classified, and no supplier can quote them for you. Your broker can, and should, before you commit to a total budget.

Certificate of Origin, and Preferential Duty

The certificate of origin states where the goods were made, and it is one of the few documents that can directly reduce your bill: under various preferential and free trade arrangements, a qualifying origin certificate lowers or removes the duty on a qualifying product. Two things to know:

  • Not every certificate is the same. A general certificate of origin and a preferential form used under a specific trade arrangement are different documents with different effects. Which one your customs will accept depends on the arrangement.
  • It has to be arranged for your country, in advance. A certificate of origin is issued against a shipment. I arrange it together with you according to your destination's requirements — that line appears in our manufacturer guide too — and it needs the consignee details to be final. Last-minute changes to the consignee are the usual reason a certificate is wrong.

Timing: the Documents That Must Start During Production

This is the section I would most like buyers to read, because it is where money actually gets lost. Freight, duty and certificates all have lead times, and only one of them is visible in a quotation. Here is the sequence that works:

WhenWhat has to happenWhat goes wrong if it slips
Before you pay a depositDestination port named; emission requirement confirmed; conformity programme identifiedYou discover after production that the engine or the certificate is wrong for your port
During productionConformity programme applied for; inspection booked; consignee details finalisedInspection cannot be scheduled before sailing; the container waits
Before sailingPhysical inspection done; certificate issued; packing list and invoice finalGoods held at origin, or arrive without the document customs will ask for
On arrivalDeclaration filed with the correct national tariff line; duty paidStorage charges that exceed the cost of the certificate you skipped

Notice the pattern: every expensive failure happens at the end, and every cheap fix happens at the beginning. A certificate that costs a modest fee and three days of paperwork during production costs demurrage and a month of delay if it is applied for after the ship sails.

The Short Version

"Do you have CE" is really three questions: what stage the engine is certified to, what machine safety conformity the model holds, and which documents your country demands per shipment. They are issued by different bodies and they matter at different moments. Our machines ship with Chinese engines built to China's non-road stage, which is what the majority of our destinations accept; if your port enforces EPA or EU certification, say so in your first message, because that changes the engine rather than the paperwork. A higher emission stage is a match to your fuel, your workshop and your regulations — not a free upgrade. Protective cab structures are demonstrated by a physical test report, not a certificate. And every expensive failure in this process happens at the port, while every cheap fix happens before the deposit: name your destination early, start conformity paperwork during production, and confirm your national tariff line with your own broker before the bill of lading is issued.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you supply a CE certificate for your backhoe loaders?
It depends which of the three things you mean. CE marking on machinery covers product safety — guarding, cab structure, noise, manuals and the technical file behind them — not engine emissions, and not the import documents your customs will ask for. Ask any supplier three things: who issued the conformity assessment and what is their identification number, does the declaration name this exact model, and can the registration number be verified with the issuing body. I would rather tell you what we hold than send a document that fails your broker's first check.
What emission stage are your engines built to, and will my country accept it?
Our machines use Chinese engines — Weichai, Yuchai and Xinchai depending on the model — built to China's non-road emission stage for new engines. Most of the markets we ship to accept that without an engine emission certificate at the border. If your country enforces EU Stage V or US EPA certification at import or registration, the machine has to be built around an engine that carries that certification, which is a different engine, price and lead time — and it cannot be arranged after the engine is built. Name your destination port in the first message and you will get a straight answer.
Is a higher emission stage always the better choice?
No. Higher stages rely on after-treatment — particulate filters, selective catalytic reduction and the sensors that manage them — which assume good fuel quality and a workshop that has seen these systems before. Where fuel quality is inconsistent or the nearest qualified technician is far away, a simpler engine is often the better machine to own. The stage should follow your country's regulation and your ground conditions, not a ladder.
Which documents have to be arranged before the ship sails?
Any pre-shipment conformity programme your destination runs — PVoC, SONCAP, SABER and similar — is applied for and inspected before sailing, and the certificate is tied to that specific consignment. The certificate of origin also has to be arranged against final consignee details. Both need to be in motion during production; applying after departure is what turns a modest fee into storage charges and delay.
What HS code should I declare, and who decides the duty?
Backhoe loaders are commonly declared under heading 8429, subheading 8429.52. The first six digits are harmonised internationally and the remaining digits are national, and it is the national line that sets your duty rate. Confirm the full line with your own customs broker before the bill of lading is issued — duty and tax are set by your country and vary by classification, so no supplier can quote them for you.
