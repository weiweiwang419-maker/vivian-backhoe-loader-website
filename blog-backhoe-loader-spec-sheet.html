How to Read a Backhoe Loader Spec Sheet — the Six Numbers That Mislead Buyers

A spec sheet looks like engineering, but it behaves like a contract: you will compare it against another sheet, and later hold it against the machine that arrives. Most of the numbers on it are true. The problem is that several of them are true only under a condition nobody printed. In this article I go row by row through the six rows where that happens most, use my own website as the worked example — including one row where two of our own pages disagreed with each other — and finish with twelve questions you can copy and send to any supplier, including me, before you pay a deposit.

Vivian — Foreign Trade Manager, AOLITE Heavy Industry
October 29, 2026 · 20 min read

A Spec Sheet Is a Negotiating Document, Not a Manual

Here is the thing I wish more buyers knew before the first quotation exchange: nobody sits down to write a spec sheet in order to deceive you. They write it to answer the questions they get asked most, in the format their domestic market expects, usually by copying the previous year's sheet and editing two rows. That is how the ambiguities get in. Not through malice — through inheritance.

Then two things happen at once. On your side, the sheet becomes the basis of a comparison between suppliers who have never agreed on a measurement convention. On the factory side, it becomes the document the workshop builds to and the sales team defends. When a machine arrives and something is 200 mm shorter than the buyer pictured, both sides are genuinely surprised, and both sides are holding the same piece of paper.

So the skill is not suspicion. The skill is one short question, asked about every important row: under what condition was this measured? Ask it six times and most of the fog clears. Ask it of every supplier and suddenly their sheets become comparable — which is the whole point.

The six rows where it matters most, in the order they cause arguments:

  1. Operating weight — 8,500 kg of what, exactly?
  2. Max dig depth — reachable in a trench, or only at one point?
  3. Dump height — loader end or backhoe end?
  4. Bucket capacity — heaped, struck, and by whose standard?
  5. Rated power — gross or net, and at what speed?
  6. Overall dimensions — working pose or transport pose?

And then there are three quiet rows — tyres, axles, transmission — that almost nobody reads and that decide whether the machine survives your roads and your mechanics. I will come to those.

Operating Weight: 8,500 kg of What, Exactly?

Two suppliers can both print 8,500 kg for a machine of the same class and both be telling the truth, because the number is a sum and nobody agreed on what goes into the sum.

The components that make two honest sheets print two different totals for a machine of the same size.

A concrete example from our own line. Our BL 80-25 is listed at 8,000 kg and the newer BL 85-25 at 8,500 kg. That extra 500 kg is not marketing rounding — the 85 carries a lead counterweight that the 80 does not, and the slewing support on the 85 is a casting rather than a fabrication. Both changes add weight deliberately, to change how the machine behaves when the backhoe is working at full reach. The number moved because the machine moved. That is the honest reason a weight figure changes, and it is the one case where a heavier number is genuinely the point.

Why the row matters beyond curiosity: weight feeds your freight quote, your trailer and lowboy choice, whether you need a permit to move it on public roads, how much pressure the stabiliser feet put into soft ground, and whether the machine can work on a suspended floor or a finished pavement. If you are comparing suppliers, ask every one of them for transport weight and operating weight as two separate lines. The supplier who can produce both without hesitation is the one who has weighed the machine rather than copied a number.

Max Dig Depth: the Number Measured at One Point

"Max dig depth 3,800 mm" is not a lie and it is not a promise about your trench. It is a single geometric extreme: the deepest point the bucket tooth can reach, usually with the machine level, the stabilisers down, the boom at its steepest, and the dipper fully curled — in other words, a narrow hole directly beside the machine.

Three conditions sit behind that number, and any of them can move it by a wide margin:

  • Standard dipper or telescopic dipper. A telescopic or extendable dipper is often listed as an option on the same sheet. If the depth figure belongs to the extended version and you buy the standard one, your trench is shallower than the sheet. Ours is a live example: on the BL 90-25 page, "Telescopic Arm" appears as a separate optional line, and the depth figure sits above it with no note about which configuration it describes.
  • Measured to the tooth tip or somewhere else. The convention is the tooth tip at the bottom of the arc, but not every sheet follows it.
  • The ground reference. Level ground, stabilisers fully planted, and the machine square to the trench. On a slope or with one stabiliser on soft fill, the reachable depth changes.

What you are actually buying is not the deepest point — it is the depth you can hold across the length of a trench. Ask for the figure with the standard dipper and, if you are digging service trenches, ask what depth the machine holds over a two-metre flat bottom.

Dump Height: Loader End or Backhoe End?

This is the row that causes the most mischief, and I can demonstrate it using two pages on this very website.

Our BL 80-25 page prints one line: Max Dump Height — 2,900 mm. Our BL 85-25 page prints two: Loader Max Dump Height — 2,670 mm and Backhoe Max Dump Height — 4,040 mm. Three numbers, two machines, one factory.

Now look at what happens in a buyer's spreadsheet. Compare the 80's 2,900 against the 85's 4,040 and the 85 looks like a different class of machine. Compare the 80's 2,900 against the 85's 2,670 and the bigger, heavier, newer machine looks worse. Both readings are wrong, and neither is the reader's fault — the fault is a row called "Max Dump Height" on a machine that has two entirely different dumping actions.

The row as printedWhat it usually meansWhat to ask
Max Dump HeightAlmost always the loader end — loading a truck. On a machine with two booms, genuinely ambiguous."Is this the loader end or the backhoe end?"
Loader Max Dump HeightFront bucket, at full lift, at a stated dump angle — measured to the bucket lip or the tooth."At what dump angle, and measured to which point?"
Backhoe Max Dump HeightWhere the backhoe bucket can drop material — often into a truck from the side."Is that with the boom over the side or straight back?"

There is a second condition hiding behind all three: the dump angle. A bucket measured at full curl and a bucket measured at a 45-degree discharge angle give different heights from the same machine, and both are legitimate. If you are loading high-sided trucks, the number you care about is the height of the bucket lip at the angle you will actually discharge at — not the geometric maximum.

I am not going to dress this up: our own sheets are inconsistent here, and I would rather say so in public than have a buyer discover it in a spreadsheet. The fix is not to make the numbers match; it is to label every one of them.

Bucket Capacity: Heaped, Struck, and Why 1.2 Is Not Twice 0.6

Bucket volume is quoted two ways. Struck is the bucket filled level with its top edge. Heaped is the same bucket with a material pile above the edge, at a defined angle of repose. The gap between them is large enough that two factories can quote the same physical bucket at two different figures without either of them lying — and large enough that a buyer comparing a struck figure against a heaped one will draw a conclusion that is simply untrue.

There is also more than one standard for how the heap is drawn, which is why a European sheet and a Chinese sheet can disagree by a visible margin on an identical bucket.

Then there is the trap inside the comparison itself. Our BL 85-25 carries a 1.2 m³ loader bucket; the BL 70-25 carries 0.6 m³. Twice the volume does not mean twice the output, because output is set by three things the bucket row does not mention:

  • Material density. A cubic metre of dry sand and a cubic metre of wet clay are different weights. The machine's lift capacity and tipping load — not the bucket volume — decide how much you can actually carry.
  • Fill factor. A bucket in blasted rock does not fill the way it fills in stockpiled topsoil. The same 1.2 m³ bucket has a different real payload in each.
  • Cycle time. A bigger bucket takes longer to fill and longer to lift. Over an hour, the faster cycle with the smaller bucket can win.

So ask one question about the bucket row — is that struck or heaped, and to which standard? — and then stop optimising it. If you want a productivity answer, ask the supplier for the rated load and the lift capacity at full height. Those two numbers, not the volume, are what tell you what the machine will carry.

Rated Power: What the Engine Code Gives Away

Here is a small decoding trick worth knowing. Our BL 85-25 is powered by a Weichai WP4G100E220, rated at 73 kW. If you did not know how to read that code, you might see "100" and assume 100 kW — and then wonder why the sheet says 73. The 100 is metric horsepower, not kilowatts. One hundred PS is roughly 73.5 kW. The engine's own model number and the rating on the same row are telling you the same fact in two units, and it looks like a contradiction until you know which unit the code uses.

That also explains something that confuses buyers comparing our range: the BL 80-25, BL 85-25 and BL 90-25 all sit at 73 kW. That is not laziness. It is one engine power platform serving three machine weights, and the differences between those machines are in the structure, the hydraulics, the axles and the counterweight — not in peak power. If you have been choosing between them on the power row, you have been reading the one row that was never going to separate them. Our comparison tool puts those machines side by side precisely so the differences that do matter are visible.

Two more conditions on the power row:

  • Gross or net. Gross is measured at the flywheel with no fan, no air filter and no alternator load. Net is measured with the engine dressed as it will actually run. Net is the lower, more honest number for a buyer, and plenty of sheets quietly print gross.
  • At what speed. Peak power arrives at one engine speed. Our BL 85-25 sheet says 73 kW at 2,200 rpm; the BL 90-25 says 73 kW at 2,400 rpm. Same peak, different part of the rev range — which changes how the machine feels in work, because what you actually drive is torque, and torque across a range.

Travel Speed, and the Other Numbers Measured at Full Throttle

Nearly every machine in our range prints a maximum speed around 35–40 km/h. Nobody digs at 40 km/h. That figure is the top of the high range, on the flat, unloaded — and on the BL 85-25 sheet it is written honestly as a pair: 5 km/h (low) / 40 km/h (high). On older sheets in the same range it is written as a single "Max Speed 40 km/h", which is technically true and practically misleading, because the range you will spend your day in is the low one.

The same pattern shows up in traction force, gradeability and hydraulic flow: each is a peak measured under one set of conditions. None of them are wrong. All of them should carry the condition with them.

Tyres, Axles, Transmission — the Three Rows Everyone Skips

Buyers read the engine, the weight and the bucket. Then the machine arrives and the first three things that cost them money are the three rows nobody read.

Tyres. A backhoe loader runs different sizes front and rear — ours are commonly 14–17.5 at the front and 19.5L–24 at the rear. A sheet that prints one size is giving you half a fact, and the half it gives you matters: tyre size sets overall width, ground pressure, ride quality on the road, and how exposed you are to punctures on demolition or quarry work. It also decides whether a local tyre shop can replace it on a Sunday.

Axles. "Heavy-duty axle" is a phrase, not a specification. Model numbers are a specification. Our BL 85-25 sheet prints the front and rear axle models; that is worth more to you than any adjective, because it tells your parts supplier exactly what to order in three years.

Transmission. A hydraulic torque converter, a powershift, and a mechanical gearbox are three different ownership experiences. On our sheets you will see a converter model — YJ280 on the BL 80-25, YJ290 on the BL 85-25 — and that model number is what your mechanic will need when something inside it wears. If the sheet says only "hydraulic transmission", ask which one.

These three rows decide whether the machine survives your ground and your workshop. If you only get to ask five questions, ask two of them here.

Overall Dimensions: the Row That Decides Your Freight Quote

Our BL 85-25 is listed at 6,470 × 2,300 × 3,000 mm. Three questions hide in that line:

  • Length in which pose? Boom folded and bucket down, or boom extended on the ground? These differ by a lot.
  • Width to what? Tyre outer edge, or including mirrors and the stabiliser feet when they are stowed? Our own sheet for that machine also lists a front bucket width of 2,300 mm, which is exactly the overall width — the bucket is the widest thing on it, and that is worth knowing before you meet a gate.
  • Height to what? Cab roof, or including the exhaust stack and beacon? Beacons come off; exhausts mostly do not.

We are not immune to the habit. One of our compact sheets prints a dimension line as 4,700 × 1,180 (1,450) × 2,400 mm — two widths, one in parentheses, and no legend saying which is which. That is precisely the failure mode this article is about: the number is there, the condition is not.

Why it lands on your invoice: the BL 85-25 page states that two units fit in one 40 ft high-cube container, and that is genuinely what lowers freight cost per machine. But that figure assumes the machine is in its transport pose, which means something on it came off or folded down to get there. Ask two follow-ups: what has to be removed or folded to reach that loading plan, and where do the removed parts travel? A supplier who answers those two questions in one message is a supplier who has loaded the container before.

The Blank Rows: What an Empty Cell Is Telling You

A dash in a cell has three possible meanings, and they are not equally worrying:

  • Nobody measured it. Common on smaller machines and on older models. It is an omission, not a concealment.
  • That configuration does not offer it. Legitimate and worth knowing.
  • Someone would rather not print it. Rare, but it happens, usually on rows where the machine is honestly behind a competitor.

I will put our own hand up again. Our comparison data used to have blanks across the older models — tyre sizes, transmissions, overall dimensions — because those sheets were built years ago from a shorter machine list and nobody ever went back to complete them. I eventually went back to the original factory parameter files on our own server and filled them in from source. Nothing was being hidden; the work had simply never been done. But a buyer looking at that table had no way to know that, and an empty cell reads the same whether it is laziness or evasion.

So: ask about every blank. One message, one line per blank. The quality of the answers will tell you more about the supplier than the numbers themselves.

Twelve Questions to Send Any Supplier — Including Me

Copy this list. Send it to everyone you are quoting, and compare the answers rather than the sheets.

  1. Give me the operating weight and the transport weight as two separate numbers, and list what each includes.
  2. Is the dig depth with the standard dipper or the telescopic one? Give me both if both exist.
  3. Is the dump height the loader end or the backhoe end? At what dump angle, measured to which point on the bucket?
  4. Are bucket capacities struck or heaped, and to which standard?
  5. Is the power figure gross or net, and at what engine speed?
  6. What is the rated load, and the lift capacity at full height?
  7. Give me the front and rear tyre sizes separately.
  8. Give me the front and rear axle models, not a description.
  9. Which transmission — make and model, not "hydraulic"?
  10. Are the dimensions in transport pose or working pose? What must be removed to reach the loading plan?
  11. How many units fit in a 40 ft high-cube container, and what has to come off?
  12. Every blank cell on your sheet: tell me whether it is unmeasured, unavailable, or something else.

Now the part that matters as much as the list — what I will and will not do with it, depending on who is asking:

What you are using the sheet forWhat I will put on itWhat I will leave off it
Comparing two suppliers on the same tableEvery figure with its measurement condition written on the same lineAny comparison table that quietly swaps my condition for theirs
Working out freight and container loadingTransport-pose dimensions, the removal list, and where the loose parts travelWorking-pose dimensions alone — they flatter the machine and misprice the freight
Matching a local competitor's published numbersStruck and heaped, both, so you can line them up either wayOnly the larger of the two, chosen because it looks better
Writing an internal tender or a rental specThe rows your workshop will need in year three — axle, transmission and tyre models"Heavy-duty" anything

That is the whole method. You are not trying to catch anyone out. You are trying to get every supplier to print the same kind of number, so that the comparison you are already doing in your head is built on something solid.

The Short Version

Most spec sheets are honest and most of their numbers are true — but six of them depend on a condition that is rarely printed: weight (what is in the sum), dig depth (which dipper and which reference), dump height (loader end or backhoe end, and at what angle), bucket capacity (struck or heaped), power (gross or net, and at what speed), and dimensions (working pose or transport pose). Behind those sit three quiet rows — tyres, axles, transmission — that decide whether the machine survives your ground and your workshop. Ask the same twelve questions of every supplier and their sheets become comparable for the first time. And take the blanks seriously: an empty cell may be laziness or it may be evasion, and you cannot tell them apart without asking. Including on my own sheets — two of the examples in this article are ours.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which number on a backhoe loader spec sheet is most often misread?
Dump height, because a backhoe loader has two dumping actions and many sheets print a single unqualified figure for both. On our own site the BL 80-25 prints one "Max Dump Height" line while the BL 85-25 prints separate loader and backhoe figures — comparing them without reading the labels produces a wrong conclusion in either direction. Always ask which end the figure belongs to, at what dump angle, and measured to which point on the bucket.
Why do two suppliers quote different weights for the same class of machine?
Because operating weight is a sum and nobody agrees on the terms of the sum. The counterweight, the buckets, a full tank of fuel and hydraulic oil, and the operator can each be in or out, and each changes the total. Ask for transport weight and operating weight as two separate lines with a list of what each includes — a supplier who has actually weighed the machine can answer immediately.
Should I compare gross or net engine power?
Net. Gross is measured at the flywheel with no fan, air filter or alternator load, so it flatters the engine; net is measured as the engine actually runs in the machine. Also check the engine speed the figure is quoted at — the same peak power at 2,200 rpm and at 2,400 rpm gives a different feel in work, because what you drive is torque across a range, not a peak.
Does a bigger bucket mean more output?
Not on its own. Output depends on material density, fill factor and cycle time as much as on volume — a large bucket in blasted rock fills and lifts more slowly than a smaller one in stockpiled topsoil. What actually limits payload is the machine's rated load and lift capacity at full height, so ask for those two figures rather than optimising the bucket volume.
How do I know whether a machine really fits in a 40 ft container?
Ask what the figure assumes. A loading plan such as "two units per 40 ft high-cube" is based on the machine in its transport pose, which means something was folded or removed to get there — and the removed parts have to travel too. Ask what comes off, where the loose parts go, and for the transport-pose dimensions rather than the working-pose ones.
